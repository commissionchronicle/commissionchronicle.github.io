Lead story

A Don Deposed? The Commission Says So

The LCN Commission says it granted permission to remove the Bonanno don. Bonanno answered with a history lesson — and did not answer this paper.

City Desk • Filed Wednesday morning

Los Santos — The claim arrived the way power now announces itself in this city: in a story, late at night. On October 16, the LCN Commission — the body that publishes for Colombo, Bonanno, Lucchese, Gambino and Chicago — stated that a Bonanno capo had deposed the Bonanno don after asking the Commission for permission, which it granted. The Commission presented it as settled. It is printed here as what it is: the Commission’s claim.

The body claiming the power is the story underneath the story. In September, by its own highlights, the same Commission removed a Gambino don and appointed his successor. In early October it published a sanctioned execution series against the Neopolo family, with Bonanno soldiers named. A table that can depose a don of one of its own five families is claiming the oldest power a commission can claim.

Bonanno’s own page does not acknowledge any of it. Its posts still style its head as Boss. On October 17, Colombo commented on this paper’s own post asserting the “legitimate LCN Commission,” and Lucchese answered in solidarity in the same thread. Outside that circle the claim is contested — Rastelli has called this body a “poser LCN commission,” and at least one other outfit claims an LCN seat it does not hold on the five’s roster.

The family’s first public movement came Sunday night, October 18: a reel captioned with the real 1984 history of a Bonanno boss moving to eliminate a capo whose power had become a threat to his leadership. The Chronicle reports the reel as the family’s movement. It is neither an acknowledgment nor a denial in words, and the deposition claim stays claimed.

From the Record — In April 1984, Cesare Bonventre, the Sicilian-born capo who co-led the Bonanno family’s feared “Zips” faction, was driven to a meeting that did not exist and shot in the car by his own family’s men, on the orders of underboss Joseph Massino — who judged him grown too powerful to sit beneath any leadership. His dismembered body was found in three steel drums in a New Jersey warehouse. Massino was charged with ordering the murder twenty years later.

The Chronicle asked the Bonanno family for its response before publication, on October 17. By the Tuesday 9:00 PM deadline, the family had not responded.

The Commission’s View

One Table, or None

The underworld of Los Santos has never lacked for ambition. This week it showed something rarer: a reach for structure. A bloc that signed a non-mafia alliance on Saturday proposed, by Tuesday, a single commission for every outfit in the city — mafia, clubs, gangs, cartels alike. A defendant proclaimed his own acquittal; the court, notably, has not proclaimed it. Two families signed a ceasefire. The pattern is not peace. It is organization.

This paper takes no side in the city’s quarrels, and it will not start now. But it will say this: cities like this one run on rules everyone can point to, or they run on whoever shot last. A single table, if it ever sits, would be the first time every power in Los Santos answered to the same roof. Already the city argues over whose table is the legitimate one — as if the argument were not itself the oldest proof that none yet is. Whether that roof holds is another question — tables built in a week have a way of burning in one.

The Chronicle will be watching. It suggests the city do the same.
